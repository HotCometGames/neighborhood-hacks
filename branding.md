Neighborhood Hacks — Website Content & Branding Guide

1. Branding Direction
Name
Neighborhood Hacks
Tagline: "Build locally, share globally"
Brand voice
Warm, not corporate. Talk to a 16-year-old like a peer, not a sponsor deck.
Grounded, not hypey. Avoid generic hackathon hype language ("disrupt," "innovate," "10x"). Say what you mean plainly.
Global but personal. Reference "your street," "your school," "your city" often — reinforce that every participant's local context is equally valid.

Visual direction
Hand-drawn brutalism + editorial collage + photocopied zine aesthetic.

The website should feel like a handmade zine or artist's sketchbook — not a polished tech startup landing page. Think: graph paper, cut-out shapes, handwritten notes, asymmetric layouts, and intentional imperfection.

Key characteristics:
- Black-and-white foundation with one acid green accent (#D7FF00)
- Graph-paper grid background (thin #252525 lines on #080808)
- Hand-cut collage elements — white shapes that look like physically cut paper
- Rough / imperfect edges — shapes aren't perfectly geometric
- Handwritten annotations — Caveat font for little labels and notes
- Tiny editorial navigation — small text in corners, not a conventional navbar
- Large empty space — don't fill every pixel
- Asymmetry — elements scattered, not neatly aligned
- Photocopy / screen-print texture — rough, imperfect lines
- Playful but sophisticated — artistic without looking childish

The important thing is not to make everything perfectly clean. The imperfections are part of the design.

Typography
Font roles:
- Space Grotesk — Display / headings / hero titles / buttons. Geometric, modern, slightly quirky.
- IBM Plex Mono — Body / UI / navigation / metadata / dates / labels. Monospaced, fits the technical sketchbook feeling.
- Caveat — Handwritten annotations only. Sparingly. Labels like "NEW →", "LOOK HERE", "MADE WITH CODE".

Font usage:
- Hero titles: Space Grotesk, bold, large
- Section headings: Space Grotesk, semibold
- Body text: IBM Plex Mono, regular
- Navigation: IBM Plex Mono, small, uppercase
- Metadata / dates / labels: IBM Plex Mono, small
- Handwritten notes: Caveat, accent color

Logo concept direction
A simple house/rooftop silhouette formed partly out of a < > code bracket or circuit-line motif — literally "neighborhood" + "hacks" in one mark
Keep it simple enough to work as a small Discord/favicon icon at 32x32px
Render in off-white (#F2F1EC) with rough/hand-drawn edges

4. About / Mission Page
Goal statement (main mission copy)
Neighborhood Hacks is a global hackathon where high schoolers use technology to solve real problems in their own communities — not hypothetical ones. We believe the most impactful builders start by looking at what's broken around them and building the fix. Over one weekend, students from every continent design, build, and pitch solutions to challenges they've personally witnessed at home — then join a global community of young builders doing the same thing, everywhere else.
Why we started this (placeholder — personalize with your own story)
Caleb and Kian started Neighborhood Hacks because we noticed something: most hackathons ask students to build for hypothetical users or abstract problems. We wanted to flip that. We wanted a hackathon where a student in a flood-prone town, a student worried about food access, or a student frustrated by their local bus schedule could all build something that actually mattered: to people they actually know.
Who this is for
Neighborhood Hacks is open to high schoolers worldwide, regardless of experience level. You don't need to have coded before, you need a real problem you care about and the willingness to try building something for it.

5. The Challenge Page
Main prompt
Pick one problem you've personally seen affect people around you — in your neighborhood, your school, or your city — and build a working solution for it.
Required submission question (explain this on the page so teams know it's coming)
As part of your submission, every team will answer: "What is the specific problem, and how do you know it's real?" — tell us what you've observed, who you've talked to, or data you've found. This is the heart of your project, so start here before you start coding.
Optional tracks (for inspiration + prize categories)

7. Judging Page
Intro copy
Every project goes through three rounds of judging — designed to be fair, thorough, and genuinely focused on impact, not just technical polish.
Round
Who Judges
What Happens
1. AI Screening
Automated
A quality check on your written submission — making sure your problem and solution are clearly explained. This isn't meant to be hard to pass, just a basic quality filter.
2. Peer Judging
A selected panel of students from partner schools and coding clubs
Panelists review submissions using our full rubric and select the top 10 projects to advance.
3. Final Round
Professional judges + student panelists, together
The top 10 submissions get a closer, more thorough review — same materials, deeper scrutiny. Judges select the Top 3 overall winners plus 2 category-specific awards.

Is this really open to students worldwide? Yes. Neighborhood Hacks is fully virtual and open to high schoolers from any country.
